You asked a freelancer and two studios what a website would cost and got three figures that are several times apart. They are hard to compare: one sent a single number, one sent a page of line items you can't make sense of, and the third wants you to fill in a brief first. Usually nobody is being greedy or naive; each of them priced a different site. "Website" covers three different products, and half the work (texts, photos, search setup, next year's hosting) often isn't in the quote at all. Here is what the price of a website in Tashkent is made of, which decisions are yours, and which costs people tend to remember too late.

## Three different products called "a website"

**A landing page** is one page for one offer: enrolment for a course, a new housing complex, a promotion at a clinic. Its job is to let someone who tapped an ad on Instagram or in a Telegram channel understand the offer in a minute and leave a request. It is enough when you have one offer and visitors come from ads. It is not enough when you sell many services and need people to find you in search for different queries: one page can't answer twenty different questions well.

**A company website** has several types of page: home, a page for each service or line of business, about, contacts, and sometimes news, vacancies and a catalogue without a cart. You need one when customers check you before they sign: a manufacturer, a construction firm, a distributor, a law firm, a private clinic. It is your business card in search and the page your sales team links to in chats.

**An online shop** adds a catalogue, cart, payment, delivery and order handling. It is no longer a shop window but part of your trade: it is tied to the warehouse, the till and the accounts, and it needs people who take and ship orders every day. It costs several times more than a company site, and the work doesn't end at launch.

| | Landing page | Company website | Online shop |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main job | A request from an ad | Trust and search | Selling and taking payment online |
| Size | One page | Usually up to 8 page types | Catalogue, cart, payment, order panel |
| Enough when | One offer, visitors from ads | Customers compare and check you | You are ready to handle orders every day |
| First version takes | 1–3 weeks | 3–5 weeks; up to 10 with custom design and a catalogue | 6–10 weeks; a full version 3–6 months |

These timelines hold only if texts, photos and access arrive on time. What usually delays a project is not the programming but waiting for materials.

If in doubt, start with the simplest thing that does the job. A landing page is enough to test demand for a new service. If orders already come in through Telegram, it can make more sense to start with a bot or a Mini App and add a web shop later.

## Design: template, adapted or custom

- **A ready-made template.** Fast and cheap, but hundreds of other sites look the same. Fine for a landing page that tests an ad, and for a small company's first site. Watch the speed: heavy themes with a dozen plugins load slowly on phones.
- **An adapted template.** A ready system of blocks, with your colours, fonts, photos and an order of blocks that fits your business. For most company sites this is the sensible middle.
- **Custom design.** Page prototypes first, then a design system, states for buttons and forms, animation. Worth it when the site is part of the brand or the product: premium property, a fashion brand, choosing a flat by floor plan, a price calculator.

What moves the design price most is the number of distinct page **types**, not the number of pages. Forty service pages on one layout are one type. Rounds of revisions, illustrations, 3D and animation add to it too. The phone version should be included in every option, not appear as a separate line.

A tip that saves time: ask for black-and-white prototypes of the main pages first. Moving a block on a wireframe takes minutes; in a finished design it takes days.

## Texts, photos and two languages

A site waits for texts more often than for a developer. A familiar picture: the design is approved, the pages are built, and the services section has been empty for three weeks because the owner has no time and nobody else knows what to write.

Decide early who writes:

- **you**: you know the business best, but you will be writing in the evenings;
- **the contractor's copywriter**: needs one or two interviews with you and someone who approves texts quickly;
- **together**: you give the facts as voice notes or bullet points, the copywriter writes, you check. This is usually the fastest route.

The Uzbek and Russian versions are two sets of texts, not a translate button. Customers spot a machine translation from Russian in the first paragraph, and it does badly in search, because people search in Uzbek with their own words, not with a literal translation of Russian queries. You need someone who writes natural modern Uzbek. Decide on the script too: Latin as the main one, Cyrillic only if your customers really read it. Each language version needs its own page addresses, so that Google and Yandex show it for searches in that language.

Photos matter more than you'd think. Stock shots of smiling strangers don't build trust; half a day photographing your office, workshop, team and products does more. A shop needs a photo of every product in one consistent style, and with a catalogue of several hundred items that is a separate job with its own timeline.

Collect before the start:

- the list of pages, with the person responsible for each text and a date;
- the logo as a vector file, and brand colours if you have them;
- photos, or a date for the shoot;
- addresses, opening hours, phone numbers, company details, map pins;
- a personal data policy, and for a shop the delivery, payment and returns terms.

Give the texts at least as much time as the design. That isn't caution; it is how it almost always goes.

## Who will edit the site

Prices, promotions, photos, vacancies and news change all the time. If every change means messaging a developer, in six months the site shows last year's prices and the phone number of a manager who has left. Your own team should be able to change prices, texts and photos without calling anyone.

The options in short:

- **A site builder.** Easy to edit and quick to launch, but limited for shops and for 1C integration, and you pay a subscription. Check where the platform stores form data: the law requires personal data of Uzbek citizens to be stored on servers in Uzbekistan.
- **WordPress or another common CMS.** Plenty of specialists and ready modules, but it needs regular updates and care with plugins.
- **A custom admin panel or a headless CMS.** Flexible and fast, and justified for a shop that syncs with 1C or has unusual logic. It costs more up front.

Before you sign, ask to see the admin panel and try changing a price, a photo and a text in both languages yourself. Ask whether there are roles (who can only suggest edits and who publishes), and whether you get a short guide and training. All admin access should be registered to your company's email, not the developer's personal one.

## What an online shop adds

A shop is a company site plus several systems, and each of them costs money and time.

- **Catalogue and filters.** You need a category structure and attributes for filters: size, colour, brand, volume. Preparing this data often takes longer than the programming: in 1C a product usually has a name and a price but no proper description or attributes.
- **Cart and checkout.** Ordering without forced registration: a phone number, an address with a pin on the map, a delivery time, a comment. The fewer fields, the fewer abandoned carts.
- **Payment.** Click, Payme, Uzum, Humo and Uzcard cards through those services, and cash or card to the courier. Your company signs the contract with each payment provider, which takes from one to several weeks, so start at once. Provider fees are separate. Fiscal receipts need an MXIK (IKPU) product code for every item: talk to your accountant early, because on a large catalogue this takes time. Installments such as Uzum Nasiya are a separate integration.
- **Delivery.** Zones by Tashkent district with their own price and minimum order, pickup, the regions, your own couriers or a connected delivery service.
- **1C sync.** Products, prices and stock flow from 1C to the site, and orders go back into 1C. Without it you sell things that aren't in the warehouse, and a manager retypes orders by hand. The contractor needs to know your 1C configuration, and you need someone on your side who can grant access.
- **Notifications and an order panel.** A new order reaches the manager in Telegram at once, the customer gets an SMS or a message about its status, and the panel shows who took the order and what stage it is at.

A shop holds customers' names, phone numbers and addresses, so hosting in Uzbekistan is not a formality here.

A good way to stay within budget: launch the first version with one payment provider, payment to the courier and the best-selling part of the catalogue. Add the rest once you see what people buy and how.

## Speed, basic SEO and analytics

Most visitors will open the site on a phone, often from a link in Telegram, which means inside Telegram's built-in browser. Test it exactly that way: on an ordinary Android phone over mobile internet. What makes sites slow is heavy video on the first screen, uncompressed photos and unnecessary plugins. Google's PageSpeed Insights in mobile mode gives a quick check.

Basic SEO is not promotion; it is careful work done once at launch:

- a unique title and description for every page in every language;
- one H1 per page and readable page addresses;
- language versions linked with hreflang, plus sitemap.xml and robots.txt;
- when you replace an old site, redirects from the old addresses to the new ones, or you lose what people already found in search;
- Google Search Console and Yandex Webmaster connected, with the sitemap submitted.

If you have a physical address, set up listings on Google Maps, Yandex Maps and 2GIS. For a café, a clinic or a neighbourhood shop, the map listing often brings more calls than the site itself.

Set up analytics (Google Analytics or Yandex Metrica) right away, with goals: form sent, phone number tapped, Telegram link opened, order placed. Tag the links in your ads with UTM parameters, so you see which channel brings requests and not just visits.

And to be honest: nobody can guarantee top positions in search for the money spent on launch. Promotion is separate, ongoing work.

## Domain, hosting and yearly costs

A site keeps costing money after launch. Ask the contractor to list these costs in writing: what, how much, and who pays whom.

- **Domain.** A .uz address is registered through an accredited registrar and renewed every year. Register it to your company and keep the registrar login yourself. An expired domain means the site and the email stop working.
- **Hosting.** A landing page or company site runs on inexpensive hosting; a shop that syncs with 1C needs its own server or cloud. If the site collects customer data, the servers must be in Uzbekistan, and local visitors get a faster site as a bonus.
- **Certificate.** Without HTTPS, browsers mark the site as not secure. Free certificates (Let's Encrypt, for example) renew automatically, but someone has to be responsible for checking that renewal works.
- **Backups.** Daily, stored somewhere else. And at least once, actually restore the site from a copy to see that it works.
- **Updates.** The CMS, plugins and server need updating: a site nobody has touched for a year is an easy target for attackers.
- **Small edits.** A new page, a Navruz banner, a changed phone number. Agree in advance: a monthly bundle of hours or payment per task.
- **For a shop**, add payment provider fees, SMS notifications, paid modules if any, and keeping the 1C sync working after 1C itself is updated.

## How to compare quotes

Ask every contractor the same questions and put the answers side by side.

1. How many page types, which ones, and what goes into each.
2. Who writes the texts, in which languages and scripts, and whether a photo shoot is included.
3. Which CMS; can we see the admin panel, and what will we be able to change ourselves.
4. How the phone version is tested: on real devices, in Telegram's browser?
5. What basic SEO includes and which analytics goals will be set up.
6. For a shop: which payment providers, who tests payments and refunds, how the 1C sync works and how often it runs.
7. Where the hosting is, who pays for it and where customer data is stored.
8. Who owns the domain, the code and every account (hosting, analytics, Search Console), and whether the source code is handed over.
9. What support after launch includes, how long the warranty lasts and what changes outside it cost.
10. Timelines by stage, and what is needed from you at each stage.

A low price is not a bad sign in itself: a template landing page to test an ad can be the right call. What matters is knowing what it includes. Be careful if the price came without a single question about your business, if the domain is registered to the contractor, if the plan has no testing, or if someone guarantees "top 3 on Google".
