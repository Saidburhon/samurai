Leads land in three managers' Telegram chats, some get written in a notebook, some in an Excel file that one person keeps. To find out how many enquiries came in last week and how many turned into payments, the owner has to call each manager. When a manager leaves, the clients leave with their phone. The opposite happens too: the company already pays for a CRM every month, but deals get entered on Friday evening from memory and the real work still happens in chats. Either way, "amoCRM or Bitrix24?" is being asked too early. Choose by how your sales actually work and by whether your team will use the system, not by the feature list. Here is how to compare the ready-made options, when building your own makes sense, and how to keep a CRM from turning into an expensive address book.

## What a CRM should do for you

For a small business a CRM is worth paying for if it does four things:

- **No lead gets lost.** Every enquiry from Telegram, Instagram, the website or a phone call becomes a record with an owner and a timestamp. You can see who hasn't been answered.
- **Every client has a next step.** Call back on Thursday, send the quote, remind them about the deposit. A deal with no dated task is usually the first one to slip away.
- **The owner sees the numbers without phoning anyone.** How many leads came in and from where, at which stage they stall, how many reached payment, how much each manager has in progress.
- **The client base stays with the company.** Contacts, chat history and agreements don't walk out with an employee.

All of this depends on one thing: the team entering data. A CRM nobody opens produces tidy, empty reports. So the main question is not which system has more features but which one your people will actually use every day.

## Describe your sales process first

The CRM copies your process, not the other way round. Start from a demo and you get someone else's pipeline from a template ("Initial contact, Negotiation, Decision") that has nothing to do with how you sell. Before you meet any integrator, spend an hour and write one page:

1. **Where leads come from** and roughly how many per day or week: Telegram, Instagram, the website, calls, referrals, trade shows.
2. **The stages from first message to payment.** For example, a hypothetical company that sells and installs air conditioners: new enquiry → needs clarified → site visit → quote sent → deposit → installation → paid in full. For each stage, write down what has to happen for the deal to move on.
3. **Who does what.** Who replies first, who prepares the quote, who goes on site, who confirms payment, who approves discounts.
4. **Where clients get lost today.** Late replies in the evening, no follow-up after the quote, nobody sure whether the client has paid.
5. **What happens after the sale.** Repeat purchases, service, warranty: does the system need to remember these?
6. **Which systems you already have:** 1C, a website, a Telegram bot, Click or Payme, telephony, e-invoices through Didox or Faktura.uz.

If the process fits into one or two pipelines of five to nine stages, almost any ready-made CRM will do, and convenience and price decide. If there are many stages, approvals, production steps, groups, subscriptions or instalments, take more care over the choice.

## amoCRM: a pipeline built around chats

amoCRM is built around the sales pipeline. Deals are cards that managers drag from stage to stage, and messages from messengers, email and website forms arrive in a shared incoming stream where they are turned into deals. Each stage can trigger an automatic action: send the client a message, set a task, reassign the deal. For a team that sells through conversations this works well, because the manager replies from the deal card and sees the whole history.

Its strengths are a fast start (basic setup takes from a few days to two weeks), an interface people pick up quickly, a mobile app, and plenty of ready integrations and integrators in Tashkent who know it. It is weaker beyond sales: company-wide tasks, documents, projects, complex work after the deal closes.

Check before you buy:

- **How Telegram connects.** Through a bot or through managers' personal accounts? Built in, or through a third-party service with its own monthly fee? Does the chat history stay if you switch that service off? Can a manager message a client first? Ask to see it working in a trial account, not in a presentation.
- **Interface language.** At the time of writing the interface is available in Russian and English, and we found no Uzbek version. For a team comfortable reading Russian that's fine. If some of your managers only work confidently in Uzbek, test it during the trial. The interface language doesn't limit the language of conversations: you can write to clients in Uzbek, Russian or anything else.
- **Price per user.** amoCRM is billed per user per month, and the plan sets the limits and how much automation you get. Count everyone who needs access, not just salespeople: the head of sales, the accountant, whoever confirms payments. Plans and prices change, so check them on the amoCRM site or with a partner at the time you buy.
- **Where it runs.** Cloud only. There is no version you can install on your own server, which matters for data storage (more on that below).

## Bitrix24: a CRM inside a bigger workspace

Bitrix24 is more than a CRM. It has tasks and projects, internal chat and video calls, a shared drive, telephony, a website builder and business process automation. The CRM inside handles leads, deals, several pipelines and automation rules on stages. A Telegram bot connects natively through Open Channels: an administrator enters the bot's token in the channel settings and client messages arrive in the CRM.

It comes in two versions:

- **Cloud.** You can start right after signing up. Plans are priced for the whole company with a user limit rather than per person, and there is a free plan with restrictions. If many people need access, this can work out cheaper than paying per user.
- **On-premise.** You buy a licence and install it on your own server, including a server in Uzbekistan. Updates and support are renewed every year. In return you need someone, on staff or a contractor, who is responsible for the server, backups and updates.

In the cloud version each employee picks their own interface language. Russian and English are available; we didn't find Uzbek in the official materials, so check the language menu in your own portal.

The main drawback for a small team is weight. Three to five salespeople who only need a pipeline end up wading through menus, notifications and sections they never use. Setup usually takes longer too, especially when a partner suggests switching everything on at once. If you choose Bitrix24 for sales, start with just the CRM and Open Channels, hide the rest, and add things as you need them.

Bitrix24 makes sense when you need tasks and communication for the whole company and not just sales, when you run several pipelines, or when the data has to sit on your own server.

## A custom CRM: when it is justified

Building your own system makes sense in a few situations:

- **The process is unusual.** For example, a training centre with groups, attendance, monthly fees per student and parents as separate contacts. Or a property developer with a unit availability chart, reservations and instalment plans. Or a distributor taking orders every day from hundreds of shops, with delivery routes and receivables. You can bend a ready-made CRM to fit, but you will be working around it every day.
- **Heavy integrations.** Two-way 1C (products, prices, stock, debts), Click, Payme and Uzum, your own app or Mini App where clients place orders themselves, telephony, e-invoicing.
- **The data must stay on your servers in Uzbekistan**, and you don't want to maintain an on-premise Bitrix24.
- **The CRM is part of your product.** Clients see their order status in a customer portal or a Telegram Mini App, and your team works on the same data.

It isn't justified when what you want is "like amoCRM, but ours": you would pay for months to rebuild what a subscription already gives you. Nor is it justified while the process is still changing. Work in a ready-made system for six months first and you will know exactly what you need.

On timeline and cost: a first working version for one documented process, with roles, a pipeline, tasks, leads from Telegram and one integration, usually takes two to four months. What moves the cost most:

- the number of roles and access rights: manager, head of sales, accountant, branch, owner;
- the number of pipelines and stage rules;
- integrations, especially with a customised 1C;
- reports, since each new report is a separate piece of work;
- whether managers on the road need a mobile app or the web is enough;
- a bilingual interface;
- moving old data out of Excel and chats;
- a server in Uzbekistan and support after launch.

Support is an ongoing cost: hosting, updates, fixes, small changes. Put it in the budget before you start, and make sure the contract says the code and the data belong to you. More in [After launch: what maintaining an app or website actually involves](/en/blog/maintenance-after-launch/).

| | amoCRM | Bitrix24 | Custom CRM |

| Built around | The sales pipeline and chats | A company workspace, with CRM as one part | Your process |
| How you pay | Per user per month | Cloud: a company plan with a user limit; on-premise: licence plus yearly renewal | Development, then support and hosting |
| Where the data is | Vendor's cloud | Vendor's cloud or your server | Wherever you decide, including Uzbekistan |

## The middle path: ready-made plus integrations

For many companies in Tashkent the sensible answer isn't ready-made or custom but both. amoCRM or Bitrix24 stays the managers' workplace, and whatever it lacks gets connected through its API:

- **Telegram bot and website.** A lead goes straight into the CRM with its source, ad tag and the client's language. Nobody retypes anything.
- **1C.** Products and prices come from 1C, a paid deal produces a document in 1C, and the client card shows any outstanding debt. More in [Connecting 1C to your website, app and Telegram bot](/en/blog/1c-integration/).
- **Click, Payme, Uzum.** The manager sends a payment link from the deal, and the payment provider's notification moves the deal to "Paid" by itself. Card-to-card transfers can't do this. More in [Accepting Click, Payme and Uzum payments](/en/blog/click-payme-uzum-payments/).
- **Your app or Mini App.** Client orders land in the same pipeline.
- **A summary for the owner.** A short Telegram message once a day or once a week with the key numbers.

One integration usually takes one to four weeks, depending on how customised your 1C is, how much data moves in each direction and how often. Decide two things up front.

First, who checks that the integrations are working. Tokens expire, APIs change, request rates are limited, and website leads can quietly stop arriving. Set up an alert: if a channel sends no leads for several working hours, someone gets a message.

Second, where the logic lives. If the rules (which manager gets a lead, when a reminder goes out) sit in a small separate backend instead of being scattered across widgets, switching CRM later means rewriting far less.

## Where the data lives and who can take it

People usually ask about this after a dispute with a former employee or contractor. Ask before you buy.

- **Physical location.** The law "On Personal Data" requires personal data of Uzbek citizens processed with information technology to be stored on servers physically located in Uzbekistan. A client's name and phone number in a CRM are personal data. Ask the vendor or partner which country the cloud account's data is physically stored in. If it isn't Uzbekistan, talk to a lawyer about how to comply: on-premise Bitrix24 on a server in Uzbekistan, your own system on local hosting, or another arrangement the lawyer confirms for your case. More in [Storing personal data in Uzbekistan](/en/blog/personal-data-in-uzbekistan/).
- **Export.** Can you export all contacts, deals, chat history and files in a usable form, whether Excel, CSV or through the API? Test it during the trial, not on the day you decide to leave.
- **Whose account it is.** Register the account to a company email address. The main administrator is the owner or a trusted employee, not the integrator and not a manager. The integrator gets a separate user you can switch off at any time.
- **Permissions.** Does a manager see only their own deals or everyone's? Can they export the database or delete contacts? For ordinary users these are usually best switched off.
- **When a manager leaves.** Access is switched off the same day and their deals and tasks are reassigned. If the manager talked to clients from a personal Telegram account, the chats and contacts leave with them. So client work should run on numbers and accounts that belong to the company and are connected to the CRM.
- **Backups**, for on-premise and custom systems: who makes them, where they are kept, and when someone last tried restoring from one.

## Getting the team to use it

CRM projects fail less often because of the wrong system than because nobody built the system into the daily work. What helps:

1. **Start small.** One pipeline, five to seven stages, as few required fields as possible: name, phone, source, next step. Every extra required field is a reason not to enter the deal.
2. **Leads arrive in the CRM by themselves.** Telegram bot, website forms, Instagram, calls: connect everything you can before launch. Managers will copy messages into cards by hand for a week, then stop.
3. **Entering data is part of the job.** "If it's not in the CRM, it didn't happen": bonuses and commission are counted only from deals in the system. The old spreadsheet closes on launch day.
4. **Every deal has a dated task.** The head of sales checks overdue tasks and deals without tasks every morning.
5. **Train on your own deals.** An hour of practice on real leads beats a presentation. Name one person inside the company whom people go to with questions.
6. **Reports the owner reads.** Not a dashboard with twenty charts, but three or four numbers once a week in Telegram: new leads by channel, how many reached payment, how many deals haven't moved in over a week, how many tasks are overdue.
7. **Review after a month.** Remove fields nobody fills in. Merge stages everyone skips. A small improvement every month is what the Japanese call kaizen, and a CRM gains a lot from it.

Common mistakes: buying licences for the whole company at once, spending two months configuring "everything" before a single manager has worked in the system, and keeping the old spreadsheet "just in case". The most common of all: the owner never opens the CRM and still asks managers for updates by phone. Then the managers soon stop keeping it up too.
