Your customers already message you on Telegram, a competitor has just launched an app, and a friend says you need a "proper website" first. That is three pieces of advice, three different budgets, and timelines from a couple of weeks to several months. You can get it wrong in either direction: pay for an app that hardly anyone installs, or go years without a website while new customers look for you on the map and find the place next door. Here is how to choose using two facts about your business: where your customers already are, and how often they come back.

## Start from the customer, not the technology

Three questions settle most cases.

1. **How do people find you the first time?** A Google or Yandex search, a map, an ad, a friend's recommendation, your Telegram channel.
2. **How often does one customer buy?** Every week (water, groceries, coffee), once a month (salon, car service), or once a year or less (a clinic for a specific problem, furniture, renovation).
3. **What phone do they use, and where do they already spend their time?** Almost all your customers have Telegram. The real question is whether they will install one more app for you, and whether their phone has room for it.

Don't answer from memory. Take last month's orders and count: how many came through Telegram, by phone, from Instagram, through delivery aggregators; how many customers ordered more than once. Ask your front-desk staff what customers ask about most. That is usually enough to see the picture.

A short rule: if first contact happens through search, you need a website; if customers already write to you on Telegram, a Mini App or a bot; if a customer comes back every week, an app is worth considering. Most small businesses end up needing two of the three, and not at the same time.

## Website: being found and trusted

Someone who doesn't know you yet types "dentist Chilanzar" or "plov delivery" into Google or Yandex, or opens Yandex Maps, Google Maps or 2GIS. A Mini App doesn't show up there. A website does, and your map listing puts the link next to your phone number and opening hours. Ads on Yandex Direct and Google usually land on a web page too, where you can see which campaign each enquiry came from.

The second job of a website is trust. Before the first call, people check your address, prices, photos and company name, and for a clinic, the doctors and the licence.

A first website doesn't need twenty pages:

- what you do and for whom, on the first screen;
- services or a catalogue with prices;
- address, map, opening hours;
- a "Message us on Telegram" button and a phone number;
- Uzbek and Russian.

A one-page site takes one to three weeks, a site with several sections three to five. It takes longer if the catalogue comes from 1C, if you want a custom design rather than a template, and most often if the texts and photos aren't ready.

A website is weak at repeat business. The customer has to remember the address and type in their phone number and delivery address again, and notifications barely work: on an iPhone, web notifications only arrive if the customer has added the site to their home screen. A site also needs upkeep: hosting, a domain, current prices. Out-of-date prices on a website are worse than none.

## Telegram Mini App: no install, already in the chat

A Mini App is a web interface that opens inside Telegram: from a bot, from a link in your channel, or from a QR code on a café table. To the customer it looks like an app, with photos, filters, a cart and payment. The main reason to build one is that the customer doesn't install anything or sign up.

What you get:

- **Sign-in without registration.** The Mini App receives the user's name and Telegram ID, and the customer can share their phone number with one tap if they agree.
- **The customer is already there.** You can pin the link in your channel, send it as a reply, or print it as a QR code on the bag.
- **Notifications as bot messages.** "Order accepted" and "courier on the way" arrive where the customer already chats. For the bot to message them, the customer has to start it or allow messages, so build that step into the flow.
- **Payments.** Click, Payme and Uzum work through a payment page, or through Telegram's built-in payments if your provider supports them.
- **One codebase for every device**, and no app store review: a fix reaches everyone at once.

A Mini App with a catalogue, cart, payment and admin panel takes roughly four to seven weeks for the first version. A button-based bot without a Mini App is faster: two to four weeks. Integration with 1C or iiko, the number of branches and delivery zones, and waiting for the payment provider contract all stretch the timeline.

The limits to know up front:

- **You depend on Telegram.** Its rules and API change, and you adapt. For example, digital goods sold inside Telegram can only be paid for in Telegram Stars. Keep your customer list with phone numbers in your own database, not only in Telegram.
- **Less screen space.** The interface lives inside Telegram's window. A catalogue and cart fit well; long forms and a ten-section account area feel cramped.
- **No storefront.** New people won't find you through search or an app store. A Mini App serves people who already know you.
- **Limited access to the phone.** Location and QR scanning are available. Working offline and background tasks such as courier tracking are not.
- **Limited broadcasts.** You can only message people who started the bot, and frequent promotional blasts get the bot blocked.

## Mobile app: for frequent, repeat customers

Does your business need a mobile app? First look at what it gives that a website and a Mini App can't:

- push notifications with your name on the lock screen, if the customer allows them;
- a loyalty card with a QR code, points and personal offers, always on the phone;
- the catalogue, order history and customer card working on a poor connection;
- full access to the phone: camera, background location for couriers, fingerprint sign-in;
- reordering in one tap with a saved address.

The cost isn't only the build:

- **Two platforms.** iOS and Android can share one codebase (Flutter, React Native), but you still test on both.
- **App stores.** An Apple developer account costs $99 a year and Google Play $25 once; a company account needs a D-U-N-S number. Every version is reviewed, and the first release, including fixes after review feedback, takes about a week.
- **Installs.** Nobody downloads an app on their own. You need a cashier who offers it, a discount on the first in-app order, ads. On a cheap phone with little storage, your app is the first thing to be deleted.
- **Upkeep.** New iOS and Android versions come out every year, store requirements change, and old versions of your app stay on customers' phones, so the server has to keep supporting them. Budget monthly, not once.

A first version for iOS and Android with a catalogue, payment, push and an admin panel takes about two to three months, plus a week for publishing. Loyalty, till integration and courier tracking add time.

An app pays off when:

- customers order weekly or more often;
- you have enough regulars for push messages and rewards to change their behaviour;
- you need phone features a Mini App can't reach;
- the app is the product itself, as with a taxi or booking service.

If a customer buys once a year, you almost never need one.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | Website | Telegram Mini App | Mobile app |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build effort | small to medium | medium | largest: two platforms plus publishing |
| Time to first version | short | medium | longest |
| How customers find it | search, maps, ads | channel, bot, link, QR code | app store, but usually because you asked them |
| What the customer has to do | open a link | open it in Telegram | install, register, grant permissions |
| Click, Payme, Uzum payments | payment page | payment page or Telegram payments | the payment provider's SDK |
| Notifications | weak | bot messages | push on the lock screen |
| Phone features | minimal | partial | full, including offline |
| Upkeep | hosting, domain, prices | server, Telegram changes | server, new iOS and Android versions, store reviews |
| Whose rules | only yours | Telegram's | Apple's and Google's |

## Typical paths

These examples are hypothetical.

**A café with delivery.** Say, a café with two branches in Yunusabad that already takes orders on Telegram. Start with a bot and a Mini App: menu, payment via Click or Payme, order alerts to the kitchen. The website is one page with the menu, address and a link to the Mini App, so people find the café on maps and in search. An app comes later, and only if you build up a group of guests who order several times a week and you want a loyalty card.

**A clinic.** Patients come once or twice a year and first find the clinic through a search like "ultrasound Mirzo-Ulugbek". The website does the main work here: doctors, services, prices and booking. Next to it, a Telegram bot confirms the appointment, sends a reminder the day before and delivers test results. Nobody installs an app for two visits a year.

**Water or grocery delivery.** Customers order every week. Start with a bot or a Mini App: quick, cheaper, and demand gets tested on real orders. Once a noticeable share of customers order regularly, ask for reminders and want to see the courier on a map, it's time for an app on the same server. The Mini App stays for those who don't want to install anything.

What to avoid:

- an app "because the competitor has one", with no plan for where the installs will come from;
- no website while new customers look for you on maps;
- three channels with three separate price lists;
- the bot, the domain and the app store account registered to the developer rather than your company.

## One backend for all three

A website, a Mini App and an app are different shop windows. Behind each one sits the same thing: catalogue and prices, customers, orders, payments, an admin panel, the exchange with 1C, iiko or amoCRM. Build each window as a separate project and you pay for that twice, prices drift apart, and points earned in the app don't show up in the Mini App.

What to ask your developer for from day one:

- a server with a documented API, so a new window can plug into it later;
- an admin panel that isn't tied to one channel;
- one customer profile keyed to the phone number, so order history and points are shared;
- payments, fiscal receipts and integrations handled once, on the server.

Where the server lives is also decided once. Under Uzbekistan's personal data law, the names, phone numbers and addresses of Uzbek citizens must be stored on servers inside the country, and that applies to all three windows.

This doesn't mean building everything now. It means not wiring the ordering logic into the bot itself. Ask your contractor: "If we add an app a year from now, what will we have to rewrite?" A good answer is: "Only the app."

## Questions to answer before you decide

Fill this in before you talk to any agency:

1. Where do new customers come from: search, maps, Instagram, recommendations, aggregators? Name the top three.
2. Where do customers order today: Telegram, phone, Instagram, Yandex Eats or Uzum Tezkor?
3. How many customers bought from you more than once last month?
4. What should the customer be able to do: see prices, book, order and pay, track a courier, collect points?
5. How do they pay: Click, Payme, Uzum, cash, a Humo or Uzcard card to the courier? Do you have a payment provider contract?
6. Where do products and prices live now: 1C, iiko, Excel? Who updates them?
7. Languages: Russian, Uzbek in Latin script, and do you need Uzbek Cyrillic?
8. Who will run it day to day: change the menu, watch orders, reply to customers?
9. How much can you spend per month on upkeep?
10. Which number, three months from now, will tell you it's working?

If the answer to question 3 is "almost nobody", you don't need an app yet, whoever recommends one.
